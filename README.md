When planning a study/trial a researcher will often need to decide on how many subjects there needs to be in the trial. This is called the power calculation. The researcher will already have a null hypothesis (H0) and an alternative hypothesis (H1).  H1 is what they are really interested in as it will be something meaningful, e.g. a clinically meaningful 10% reduction in the rates of mortality in a disease after the administration of a treatment.  
To conduct the power calculation there also needs to be two parameters chosen, alpha and power (1-beta).  Alpha is typically small, often 0.05 and power large, typically 0.8 or 0.9.  These are arbitrary and I have yet to find anyone that can justify their choice.
This page shows that they can be estimated given the prior probability that the trial intervention is positive.  If the researcher has no clue, this may be 0.5.  But, if it is a notoriously difficult disease (e.g.) to treat and most trials fail, then this could be 0.1 or less.  On the other hand if there is good biological plausibility, pre-clinical evidence, and pilot data then this probability may be much higher than 0.5.
What is crucial is that the researcher thing first about what risk they want to take in being wrong if the result of the trial is positive.  The   P(R+ | H1) number maybe thought of as the probability that a positive test really indicates a beneficial intervention (i.e. H1). I would expect this to be well over 0.5 (50%) for most studies. The researcher having entered their prior probability may adjust alpha and power to make this what they would be satisfied with.  Then, they can go to their statistician and explain why they want to use a particular alpha and power to work out how many are needed in the study.
